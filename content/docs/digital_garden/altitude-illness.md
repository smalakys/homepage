# Altitude Illness: AMS, HACE and HAPE

Acclimatization is the body catching up with the mountain. Altitude illness is what happens when it doesn't. The three named conditions — acute mountain sickness, high altitude cerebral oedema and high altitude pulmonary oedema — are not separate diseases that strike at random. They are the predictable failure modes of the adaptations described in the [acclimatization](../acclimatization/) article, and each one has a clear early signal and a simple, effective treatment.

The difficulty is almost never the medicine. Descent, oxygen, dexamethasone and nifedipine are all long-established interventions with strong evidence behind them. What kills people is the **decision** — specifically, the decision to keep ascending with symptoms.

> [!CAUTION]
> This article contains specific drugs and doses because you cannot understand the guidelines without them. **It is not medical advice, and it does not replace training.** Every drug here has contraindications and interactions. Get your medications, doses and expedition medical plan from a clinician who knows your history and your itinerary, and get proper wilderness first aid training before you need it.

## 1. The Single Rule That Matters

Every consensus body says the same thing in near-identical language, so it is worth stating before anything else.

**Wilderness Medical Society, 2024:** people with AMS of any severity or HACE should cease ascending. Further ascent or re-ascent to a previously attained altitude should **never** be undertaken if there are ongoing symptoms.

**UIAA Medical Commission:** no further ascent with symptoms. Stay at the same altitude until symptoms have **completely** disappeared. Descend if symptoms do not improve, or worsen, within 24 hours. And the framing principle that covers everything else:

> All symptoms at altitude must be considered as altitude related until proven otherwise.

The commonly cited "golden rules," usually attributed to Peter Hackett, compress it further:

1. It is fine to get altitude illness. It is not fine to die from it.
2. Any illness at altitude is altitude illness until proven otherwise.
3. Never ascend with symptoms.
4. If you are getting worse, go down at once.

> [!WARNING]
> The reason rule three is absolute is mechanistic. HACE occurs **usually in a person who already has AMS**, and typically appears 24–72 hours after a gain in altitude. AMS is not merely unpleasant — it is the precursor state. Ignoring it and sleeping higher is the specific behaviour that converts a self-limiting illness into a medical emergency.

## 2. Acute Mountain Sickness

### What it is

AMS is a **symptom-based clinical diagnosis**. There is no blood test, no imaging finding, and no oxygen saturation threshold that makes or excludes it. That is uncomfortable for people who want a number, but it is the reality.

### The 2018 Lake Louise score

The Lake Louise score was substantially revised in 2018. Each of four items is scored 0–3:

| Item | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Headache** | None | Mild | Moderate | Severe, incapacitating |
| **Gastrointestinal symptoms** | Good appetite | Poor appetite or nausea | Moderate nausea or vomiting | Severe nausea and vomiting, incapacitating |
| **Fatigue / weakness** | Not tired or weak | Mild | Moderate | Severe, incapacitating |
| **Dizziness / light-headedness** | None | Mild | Moderate | Severe, incapacitating |

**Diagnosis requires a headache, a total score of at least 3, and at least 6 hours at the new altitude.** Severity bands: 3–5 mild, 6–9 moderate, 10–12 severe.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The most consequential change in 2018 was the **removal of sleep disturbance** from the score. Disturbed sleep at altitude is now regarded as a normal consequence of hypoxia rather than evidence of illness. This is genuinely liberating for climbers: **a bad night at 4,000 m is expected and is not, on its own, a reason to worry — whereas a headache is.**

### How common is it?

| Altitude | Approximate AMS incidence |
|---|---|
| 1,920–2,960 m (Colorado tourists) | ~25% |
| 2,500–3,000 m | 10–30% |
| 4,559 m, fast unstaged ascent | 31–58% depending on susceptibility |
| 4,300 m, direct ascent | ~90% in one controlled study |

HAPE is rare below 3,000 m and HACE is essentially not seen below 4,000 m, but AMS starts at moderate elevations that many people do not think of as "altitude" at all.

### Timing and course

Onset is typically **6–12 hours** after arriving at a new altitude, peaking at 24–48 hours. With no further ascent, mild AMS usually resolves over one to two days as acclimatization catches up. That is why a rest day at the same altitude is a legitimate treatment and not a defeat.

### What to do

- **Stop ascending.** This is the treatment, and it is free.
- **Rest at the same altitude until completely symptom-free.**
- Treat the headache with **ibuprofen 600 mg every 8 hours** or paracetamol if you want, but understand what you are doing (see below).
- **Acetazolamide 250 mg every 12 hours** speeds acclimatization.
- **Descend** if symptoms worsen, or fail to improve within 24 hours, or if any neurological or respiratory signs appear.

> [!WARNING]
> Analgesia relieves the headache without touching the underlying maladaptation — and it removes your best early-warning signal. A headache that responds to ibuprofen does **not** license further ascent. The same logic applies more strongly to dexamethasone, which suppresses symptoms without accelerating acclimatization at all.

## 3. High Altitude Cerebral Oedema

### What it is

HACE is the severe end of the same spectrum as AMS: hypoxia-driven cerebral vasodilation combined with a leaky blood-brain barrier, producing brain swelling. It is uncommon — roughly **0.5–1%** of people ascending above 4,000–5,000 m — and it is rapidly fatal without treatment.

It usually develops in someone who already has AMS, and progression from mild symptoms to coma has been described within **12–24 hours**.

### The sign that matters: ataxia

The earliest and most sensitive neurological sign is **ataxia** — loss of coordination, most obvious in gait.

> [!TIP]
> **The tandem gait test.** Have the person walk about four metres in a straight line, heel directly in front of toe, arms at their sides. Marked swaying, stepping off the line, or inability to do it at all is a red flag. Test people when they are well, early in a trip, so you know what their normal looks like.
>
> This test takes fifteen seconds, needs no equipment, and is far more informative than any pulse oximeter reading.

Other features: severe headache, confusion, disorientation, vomiting, irrational behaviour, and progressive decline in consciousness through stupor to coma.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Above 8,000 m, the classic warning signs do not appear.** In an analysis of 94 deaths above 8,000 m on Everest, the recorded symptoms in non-survivors were profound fatigue (34 cases), confusion or coma (21), and ataxia (12). **Headache: zero cases.** At extreme altitude, ataxia and impaired consciousness arrive *before* headache and nausea, not after. Watch for the climber who falls behind, stumbles, or stops making sense.

### Treatment, in order of priority

1. **Descend immediately.** 500–1,000 m, or until symptoms clearly improve. This is the only definitive treatment.
2. **Dexamethasone 8 mg** once (oral, intramuscular or intravenous), then **4 mg every 6 hours**.
3. **Supplemental oxygen**, targeting a saturation above 90%.
4. **Portable hyperbaric chamber (Gamow bag)** if descent is impossible — it simulates a descent of roughly 1,500 m and buys time. It does not replace going down.

A person with HACE cannot be relied on to walk safely or make decisions. Plan on assisting or carrying them.

## 4. High Altitude Pulmonary Oedema

### What it is

HAPE is the leading cause of death from altitude illness, and mechanistically it has nothing to do with the brain.

The lungs respond to low alveolar oxygen with **hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction** — narrowing of small pulmonary arteries. At sea level this is useful, diverting blood away from poorly ventilated lung regions. At altitude, where the *whole* lung is hypoxic, it just raises pulmonary artery pressure.

In susceptible people this response is both **exaggerated and uneven**. Some regions constrict harder than others, so the less-constricted capillary beds take the full brunt of the raised pressure. Beyond a threshold the capillary walls physically fail — John West's **capillary stress failure** — and protein-rich fluid, sometimes with red blood cells, leaks into the alveoli.

> [!NOTE]
> This is why HAPE is described as **non-cardiogenic** pulmonary oedema. The heart is fine. The problem is a pressure-driven mechanical leak in the lung's own plumbing, and the inflammation that follows is a consequence, not a cause.

### Incidence and timing

Roughly **2–6%** of unacclimatized people ascending rapidly above 2,500–3,000 m. It typically develops **1–5 days after arrival**, with the **second night** at a new altitude being the classic moment.

### Risk factors

- **Prior HAPE.** This is the big one. Recurrence in susceptible individuals ascending rapidly to 4,559 m has been reported around **60%**.
- **Rapid ascent** and high sleeping altitude.
- **Heavy exertion**, which raises pulmonary artery pressure further.
- **Cold** — universally taught, physiologically plausible, though not well quantified in guidelines.
- Structural causes such as a small pulmonary vascular bed, or unilateral absence of a pulmonary artery, which can cause HAPE at surprisingly low elevations.

### Early warning signs

This is where HAPE is beatable, because the early picture is distinctive:

- **A dry cough**
- **Breathlessness out of proportion** to the effort, and to everyone else in the party
- **A clear drop in exercise performance** — being unable to keep up on ground you managed yesterday
- **Delayed recovery** after exertion
- **Resting oxygen saturation markedly lower than your companions at the same altitude**

Later: breathlessness at rest, crackles in the chest, rapid heart rate, cyanosis, low-grade fever, and eventually a wet cough with pink frothy sputum.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The single most useful discriminator is **hypoxaemia out of proportion to that expected for the elevation**. This is the one place where a pulse oximeter genuinely earns its place — not for screening healthy people, but for evaluating someone who already looks unwell. "Everyone here is 84–88 and he is 68, and he is breathless and coughing" is a real signal. "I'm 88 and she's 91" is noise.

### Distinguishing HAPE from a khumbu cough

Almost everyone develops a harsh dry cough at altitude, from ventilating enormous volumes of cold dry air. Telling it apart from early HAPE matters:

| | Khumbu cough | HAPE |
|---|---|---|
| Cough | Dry, harsh, painful | Dry early; later wet, frothy, sometimes pink |
| Breathlessness | Not disproportionate | **Out of proportion** to peers and to your own prior form |
| Exercise performance | Preserved | **Clearly reduced** |
| Saturation | Normal for the altitude | **Low for the altitude** |
| Fever | None | Mild fever common |

Note the asymmetry: absence of fever supports a simple cough, but **its presence settles nothing** — fever does not exclude HAPE, and pneumonia is also on the list.

### Treatment, in order of priority

1. **Descend.** The definitive treatment. Minimise exertion during descent if at all possible — exertion raises pulmonary pressure further.
2. **Supplemental oxygen**, targeting saturation above 90%. Oxygen alone can be dramatically effective because it directly relieves the hypoxic vasoconstriction driving the whole process.
3. **Nifedipine ER 30 mg every 12 hours** (or 20 mg every 8 hours) as an adjunct if descent and oxygen are not available.
4. **Portable hyperbaric chamber** if descent is impossible.

> [!WARNING]
> **Dexamethasone is not a treatment for HAPE**, and neither drug should ever be a reason to delay descent. Over-the-counter oxygen canisters and oxygen bars have **no role** in HAPE treatment.

## 5. The Drugs, in One Place

These are the Wilderness Medical Society 2024 regimens.

| Drug | Prevention | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Acetazolamide** | AMS: 125 mg every 12 h (consider 250 mg every 12 h for high-risk profiles) | AMS: 250 mg every 12 h |
| **Dexamethasone** | AMS: 2 mg every 6 h or 4 mg every 12 h | AMS: 4 mg every 6 h. HACE: 8 mg once, then 4 mg every 6 h |
| **Nifedipine ER** | HAPE (susceptible only): 30 mg every 12 h or 20 mg every 8 h | HAPE: same |
| **Tadalafil** | HAPE (susceptible only): 10 mg every 12 h | — |
| **Ibuprofen** | Second-line for AMS | High-altitude headache: 600 mg every 8 h |

Key points that are easy to miss:

- **Acetazolamide accelerates real acclimatization.** Dexamethasone does not — it suppresses symptoms. That difference dictates how each is used.
- **The appropriate acetazolamide dose above 5,000 m "is not clear"**, in the guidelines' own words. If you are going to a 6,000 m peak, that uncertainty is yours.
- **Do not combine nifedipine and tadalafil.** The guidelines state there is no role for concurrent use.
- Acetazolamide is **contraindicated** after anaphylaxis to a sulfonamide or a history of Stevens-Johnson syndrome, and it is on the **WADA prohibited list**.
- Dexamethasone used beyond 5–7 days needs a taper, and abrupt cessation risks rebound.

## 6. Ascent Rate: The Only Prevention That Really Works

Everything above is treatment. The prevention is arithmetic.

**Wilderness Medical Society 2024:**

- Above **3,000 m**, do not increase **sleeping elevation** by more than **500 m per day**
- Include a **rest day every 3–4 days**
- Coming from low altitude, sleep one night at an intermediate elevation before going higher

**UIAA** is slightly more conservative: 300–500 m per day increase in sleeping altitude above 2,500 m, with a rest day every 3–4 days.

> [!TIP]
> Note that both rules govern **sleeping altitude**, not your day's high point. "Climb high, sleep low" works precisely because the number that matters is where you spend the night. A day trip to 5,000 m followed by a night at 4,200 m is a very different exposure from sleeping at 5,000 m.

The evidence that this dominates everything else is worth repeating from the [individual variability](../individual-variability/) article. In a study of 827 mountaineers at 4,559 m:

| | Fast ascent, no pre-exposure | Both slow ascent and pre-exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Susceptible individuals | **58%** | **7%** |
| Non-susceptible individuals | **31%** | **4%** |

Susceptibility roughly doubles risk. **Ascent strategy moves it by a factor of eight.**

## 7. Descent: How Much, and When

**How much:** typically **500–1,000 m**, or until symptoms clearly resolve. There is no fixed number, because what matters is reaching an altitude your body can cope with.

**When:**

- Any **neurological** sign — ataxia, confusion, altered consciousness — is immediate, non-negotiable descent.
- Any suspicion of **HAPE** is immediate descent.
- AMS that **worsens**, or fails to improve within **24 hours** at rest, is descent.
- Anything you cannot confidently explain as something other than altitude illness is descent.

**Re-ascent** is permissible only once the person is **completely symptom-free off medication**. Being symptom-free *on* dexamethasone does not count.

> [!CAUTION]
> Descent at night, in bad weather, or over technical ground carries its own serious risks, and the decision is not always simple. This is exactly why the guidelines emphasise acting on **early** symptoms — the point at which descent is easy and safe — rather than waiting for certainty. By the time the diagnosis is obvious, the descent has usually become dangerous.

## 8. Pulse Oximetry: Useful, and Widely Misused

Saturation readings look objective, which is precisely the problem.

**Roughly what to expect** in healthy, rested, reasonably acclimatized people. Treat these as ranges, never as targets:

| Altitude | Typical resting saturation |
|---|---|
| ~2,700–3,000 m | 90–94% |
| ~3,500 m | 89–91% |
| ~4,000 m | 83–90% |
| ~4,500 m | 80–87% acclimatized; as low as 70–72% in the first hours |
| ~5,000–5,400 m | 80–85% |
| ~6,400 m | ~72% |

**Why absolute values mislead:**

- **You are on the steep part of the oxyhaemoglobin curve.** A few mmHg of arterial oxygen moves saturation several points. Measure at rest, breathing quietly, for several minutes, and take the predominant value — not the first number on the screen.
- **Healthy people differ enormously.** There is close to a **ten-fold range in hypoxic ventilatory response** between healthy individuals, so two equally acclimatized people at the same camp can legitimately differ by many points.
- **The reading changes with time at the same altitude.** At the Capanna Margherita, the same cohort read **70% at four hours** after arrival and **78% on the morning of day four**. A number without a timestamp means nothing.
- **Device accuracy collapses below 80% saturation** — exactly where you are reading above 4,500 m. Many oximeters are also rated only to 3,000–3,700 m operating altitude, and to temperatures well above what you get on a cold morning.
- **Cold fingers, bright snow and a poor probe fit** all corrupt readings. Warm the hand, shield it, use a finger rather than an earlobe or toe.
- **Carbon monoxide falsely raises the reading**, which matters if you have been cooking in a tent vestibule.

> [!WARNING]
> The strongest argument against routine screening is that **a reassuring number can kill someone**, because it licenses continued ascent in a symptomatic person. The guidance is explicit: avoid routine screening of healthy people for altitude illness or summit potential, and avoid decisions based on small differences between individuals or over time.
>
> Use an oximeter to **evaluate someone who is already unwell** — especially to confirm suspected HAPE — and to **titrate supplemental oxygen**. Not to screen a healthy party.

Consumer wrist-based readings are markedly less accurate than fingertip oximetry, and both have documented accuracy bias in people with darker skin pigmentation.

## 9. Things That Are Not Altitude Illness (But Look Like It)

Even though the governing rule is to assume altitude illness until proven otherwise, it helps to know the mimics:

- **Dehydration** — thirst, low mood, headache, low urine output, dry mouth
- **Exhaustion** — and note that the treatment overlaps: rest
- **Hangover** — diffuse headache, fatigue, nausea, no motivation. The UIAA considers hut drinking a serious enough diagnostic confounder to give it its own row alongside stroke and meningitis
- **Hyponatraemia** from overdrinking — headache, nausea, confusion, and the treatment is the *opposite* of what you would do for dehydration
- **Carbon monoxide poisoning** from stoves — headache and confusion, and notably **no cyanosis**
- **Sunstroke**, meningitis, caffeine withdrawal, hyperventilation syndrome
- **High-altitude headache** as a standalone diagnosis: bilateral, mild-to-moderate, worse on exertion, above 2,500 m, resolving within 24 hours of descending — headache *without* the AMS symptom cluster

Two more that are benign but alarming:

**Peripheral oedema** — puffy face, eyelids, hands and ankles — is common, more so in women, and resolves on descent. Importantly, it has **no significant association with AMS**. Swollen hands do not mean you are getting sick, and their absence does not mean you are safe. Take your rings off early.

**High-altitude retinal haemorrhage** is common above 4,000 m, usually completely asymptomatic, found only on eye examination, and resolves over days to weeks. The exception that matters: a haemorrhage involving the macula causes a painless **central blind spot or blurred central vision**. That warrants descent and an eye examination.

## 10. Five Myths Worth Killing

**"Being fit protects you."** It does not. Training status was non-significant in an 827-mountaineer study, and in Richalet's risk-prediction score, **regular physical activity actually increases predicted risk** — probably because fit people ascend faster and push harder. Fitness determines what you can do at altitude, not whether you get sick.

**"I was fine last time, so I'll be fine again."** Prior tolerance is a favourable signal, but the guidelines note that a history of altitude illness does not necessarily predict problems on all future ascents, because a slower profile changes the outcome. The inverse holds too. Most repeat altitude illness is repeat *behaviour*.

**"Drink loads of water."** Forced or over-hydration is explicitly listed among interventions **not** shown to be of benefit, and overdrinking causes hyponatraemia, whose symptoms mimic AMS and whose treatment is the opposite. Drink to thirst. Urine colour does not indicate hydration status at altitude, though a volume of at least a litre a day is a reasonable check.

**"A beer at the hut is fine."** Alcohol is a respiratory depressant that worsens nocturnal hypoxaemia and periodic breathing, its after-effects are clinically indistinguishable from AMS, and it degrades exactly the judgement that is your safety system. The same concern applies to sedatives and opiates. The guideline-endorsed answer to bad sleep at altitude is not a sleeping pill — it is **acetazolamide**, which treats the underlying periodic breathing.

**"Coca tea / an oxygen can / this patch will sort me out."** The 2024 guidelines dispatch the whole category in one sentence, listing as unproven: coca leaves and coca tea, forced hydration, oxygen bars and over-the-counter oxygen canisters, and other over-the-counter products such as powdered drink mixes, patches and oral supplements.

## The Bottom Line

- **AMS is a symptom diagnosis, and headache is the anchor.** Score yourself honestly — four items, 0–3 each, three points and a headache after six hours at altitude.
- **Bad sleep is not AMS.** It was removed from the score in 2018 because disturbed sleep at altitude is simply hypoxia doing its normal thing.
- **Ataxia is the sign that turns a bad evening into an emergency.** Learn the tandem gait test and use it.
- **The early HAPE signal is breathlessness and lost performance disproportionate to everyone else**, not a dramatic cough. By the time there is pink sputum you are very late.
- **Above 8,000 m, headache and nausea are not reliable heralds.** Watch for confusion, stumbling, and falling behind.
- **The treatment for all three is descent.** Drugs and oxygen buy time; they do not substitute.
- **The prevention for all three is ascent rate.** Five hundred metres of sleeping altitude a day above 3,000 m, with a rest day every three or four. It moves risk eightfold — more than any drug, tent, or amount of fitness.

Everything else in altitude medicine is detail hung off that last point.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I tell an ordinary headache from AMS?

You often can't with certainty, and the guidelines tell you not to try — treat any symptom at altitude as altitude-related until proven otherwise. What you can do is score it. AMS requires a headache **plus** a total Lake Louise score of at least 3 across headache, gastrointestinal symptoms, fatigue and dizziness, after at least six hours at the new altitude. A lone mild headache with a normal appetite, normal energy and no dizziness is more likely high-altitude headache, which is a real but separate diagnosis and usually responds to ibuprofen.

The practical distinction is what you do next. A headache alone, responding to analgesia, with no other symptoms, does not obviously require descent — but it absolutely does mean you should not gain sleeping altitude that night. Add nausea, unusual exhaustion or dizziness and you are in AMS territory, and the stop-ascending rule applies without argument.

### Should I carry dexamethasone even if I don't plan to use it?

For any serious high-altitude objective, most expedition medical plans include it, and the reason is that dexamethasone is the drug that lets someone with HACE walk down. That is a genuinely life-saving use, and it is not a decision you want to be making without the drug in your bag.

But be clear about what it is for. It does not accelerate acclimatization, it masks symptoms rather than fixing them, and being symptom-free on dexamethasone does not mean you are well enough to go higher. It is a drug for getting down. The guidelines reserve prophylactic use for genuinely high-risk situations like rescue teams airlifted above 3,500 m. Carry it, understand it, get the dose and the plan from a clinician before you leave, and hope it stays in the bag.

### Is a Gamow bag worth carrying?

On expeditions where descent may be impossible for hours — bad weather, technical terrain, a remote base camp — yes, and many organised expeditions carry one. It works: pressurising to around 2 psi simulates a descent of roughly 1,500 m, and that can be enough to turn a deteriorating HACE or HAPE patient around long enough to move them.

The caveats are practical. It is bulky and heavy, it requires someone to pump continuously, the patient is sealed inside and cannot be examined or easily communicated with, and anyone claustrophobic will find it very hard. Most importantly, it is a bridge, not a destination — it buys time to organise a descent, and time spent in the bag is only useful if it is being used to arrange going down.

### What if descending isn't safe — bad weather, night, technical ground?

This is the genuinely hard case, and the honest answer is that it is a judgement call with no clean rule. Descending at night down a glacier in a whiteout carries real risk of killing the whole party, and that has to be weighed against the trajectory of the patient.

Two things help. First, if you are in this position for HACE or HAPE, use everything you have while you wait: oxygen targeting saturation above 90%, dexamethasone for HACE, nifedipine for HAPE, a hyperbaric bag if you have one. These can stabilise someone through a night. Second, and more usefully, this scenario is precisely why the guidelines push you to act on *early* symptoms. The window where descent is easy and safe is also the window where the diagnosis is uncertain. Waiting for certainty is how you end up choosing between two bad options in the dark.

### My oximeter says 78% at 4,800 m. Should I go down?

On that number alone, no. That reading is within the plausible range for that altitude, especially if you arrived recently, and the device is operating near or below the saturation where its accuracy is validated. The guidance is explicit about not making decisions based on small differences from other people or from earlier readings.

What should drive your decision is how you feel and how you are functioning. Score yourself on the Lake Louise items. Do the tandem gait test. Ask whether you are breathless out of proportion to your companions, and whether your performance has fallen off compared with yesterday. If you are symptom-free and moving normally, the number is noise. If you are the one person in camp reading dramatically below everyone else *and* you are coughing and breathless, that is the one situation where the oximeter is doing real work — and it points at HAPE, which means going down now.
