# Pre-acclimatization: What You Can Actually Do Before You Leave

There is a large industry built on the promise that you can arrive at altitude already adapted. Hypoxic tents, altitude generators, "intermittent hypoxic training" packages, oxygen cans, powders, patches. Some of it works. Most of it does not work at the doses people actually use, and the single most effective intervention is also the cheapest and least glamorous.

This article separates the three categories: what has good evidence, what has plausible evidence at doses almost nobody uses, and what has none.

> [!NOTE]
> This is the fifth entry in a series on altitude. It assumes the mechanisms covered in [acclimatization](../acclimatization/) and the illness definitions in [altitude illness](../altitude-illness/).

> [!CAUTION]
> This article discusses prescription medicines and specific doses because you cannot evaluate the evidence without them. It is not medical advice. Every drug here has contraindications and interactions, and doses must come from a clinician who knows your history and your itinerary.

## 1. What Pre-acclimatization Is Actually Trying to Buy

Be clear about the goal, because it is not the same as the goal of altitude training for athletes.

**Athletes** use altitude to raise haemoglobin mass for sea-level race performance. That is the Live High Train Low paradigm, and it is optimised for a marginal gain in oxygen-carrying capacity.

**Mountaineers** want something different: **ventilatory acclimatization** (breathing more, and more stably), **acid-base adjustment** (the kidney response that lets you keep hyperventilating), and **reduced risk of altitude illness**. A marginal haemoglobin gain is almost irrelevant by comparison.

Conflating these two goals is the most common error in the popular altitude-tent literature, and it explains why so much of the marketing cites athletic studies for a mountaineering problem.

## 2. The Vocabulary, Decoded

The terminology is genuinely confusing, and vendors exploit that.

**By how the low oxygen is produced:**

| Type | How it works | Barometric pressure | Oxygen fraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Normobaric hypoxia** | Nitrogen dilution or membrane oxygen filtration feeding a tent, room or mask | Normal (sea level) | Reduced |
| **Hypobaric hypoxia** | Altitude chamber, or actual altitude | Reduced | Normal (20.9%) |

Both lower inspired oxygen pressure. The traditional assumption is that equal inspired oxygen pressure means equal physiological stimulus — and that assumption is now genuinely contested.

**By exposure pattern:**

- **IHE (Intermittent Hypoxic Exposure)** — short passive exposures at rest, typically 1–4 hours a day, sometimes in a cyclic five-minutes-on, five-minutes-off "hypoxicator" format.
- **IHT (Intermittent Hypoxic Training)** — exercising *in* hypoxia. Sessions of 30–90 minutes, a few times a week.
- **LHTL (Live High, Train Low)** — sleep or reside at moderate altitude for 12+ hours a day, train low to preserve training intensity. The dominant athletic paradigm.
- **LHTH (Live High, Train High)** — the classic altitude camp.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Whether normobaric and hypobaric hypoxia are equivalent is an active scientific dispute. Millet and colleagues argue that at the same inspired oxygen pressure, hypobaric hypoxia produces different ventilation patterns, fluid balance and oxidative stress. Richalet argues the differences dissolve once you control for temperature, humidity, exposure duration and carbon dioxide washout. A systematic review of 13 crossover trials (Coppel and colleagues, 2015) found most variables similar, with small, inconsistent, confounded differences in ventilation and nitric oxide.
>
> The practical resolution: **duration of exposure matters far more than the modality.** Do not conclude a tent is worthless because it is normobaric. The problem with tents is dose, not pressure.

## 3. Dose Is Everything

The most quotable statement in the field comes from the **Wilderness Medical Society 2024 guidelines**, which decline to endorse any specific pre-acclimatization protocol and add:

> With regard to normobaric hypoxia exposure in a hypoxic tent, only sufficiently long exposures (> 8 hours per day) that can be undertaken regularly over an appropriate number of weeks are likely to offer any significant preacclimatization benefit.

**More than 8 hours a day, for weeks, or don't bother.** That single sentence eliminates the majority of commercially sold protocols.

What the underlying evidence looks like:

| Intervention | Effect on AMS | Evidence quality |
|---|---|---|
| Staging 6 days at 2,200 m before 4,300 m | Incidence **91% → 45%** | Good (small, crossover) |
| IHE 4 h/day × 7 days at simulated 4,300 m | Significant reduction | Moderate |
| IHE 3 h/day × 5 days plus exercise | Reduced *severity* only; incidence not significant | Weak |
| Hypoxic tent >8 h/night for weeks | Plausible, not quantified | **Weak — no RCT exists** |
| IHE under 2 h/day, or cyclic "hypoxicator" | No credible evidence | Absent |
| IHT (exercise in hypoxia) | No credible evidence for AMS | Absent |

Burtscher and Millet have proposed a working framework of roughly **100–200 cumulative hours** of hypoxic exposure above 2,000–3,500 m. Treat that as expert framing, not a validated threshold.

> [!WARNING]
> Short cyclic sessions — an hour a day, or five-minutes-on/five-minutes-off protocols totalling under 90 minutes — are the format most heavily marketed and have **no convincing evidence** of reducing altitude illness on a subsequent ascent. Neither does training in hypoxia, whose exposure durations are far below any plausible acclimatization dose.

## 4. Staged Ascent: The Best-Evidenced Option, and It's Free

This is where the numbers are strongest, and it is the section that should change your behaviour.

**Beidleman and colleagues (2009)** took eleven sea-level residents to 4,300 m twice — once directly, once after **six days staged at 2,200 m**:

- AMS incidence fell from **91% to 45%**
- AMS severity score fell from 1.05 to 0.59
- Resting arterial saturation at 4,300 m rose from roughly **80% to 83%**, with lower end-tidal carbon dioxide confirming genuine ventilatory acclimatization

**Schneider and colleagues (2002)** studied 827 mountaineers ascending to the Capanna Margherita at 4,559 m. This one table does more work than any other in the literature:

| | Fast ascent, no pre-exposure | Pre-exposure only | Slow ascent only | Both |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Susceptible individuals** | **58%** | 29% | 33% | **7%** |
| **Non-susceptible individuals** | **31%** | 16% | 11% | **4%** |

Read the corners. A susceptible person on a fast unstaged ascent has a 58% chance of AMS. The same person, ascending slowly with pre-exposure, has **7%**. Age, sex, training status, BMI, alcohol and smoking were all non-significant.

> [!TIP]
> Arriving in Kathmandu, Cusco or Leh and spending **3–7 days at 2,200–3,000 m before the trek starts** is the highest-yield, lowest-cost, best-evidenced pre-acclimatization intervention available. It is better evidenced than any tent protocol, and it costs nothing but time.

The WMS version of the same principle, for shorter trips: a sea-level resident travelling to a resort above 2,800 m should spend one night at an intermediate elevation such as Denver (1,600 m). And once above 3,000 m: **do not increase sleeping elevation by more than 500 m per day, with a rest day every 3–4 days.**

## 5. Hypoxic Tents: An Honest Assessment

Tents are not useless. They are oversold, and the protocols circulating online are mostly written by the people selling them.

**What the evidence actually is.** There is **no randomised controlled trial** showing that a hypoxic-tent protocol reduces altitude illness on a subsequent Himalayan or Andean expedition. The WMS explicitly declines to endorse a protocol. When you search for peer-reviewed tent protocols, the results are dominated by vendor content.

**The core tension.** The dose that would plausibly help — more than 8 hours a night at a meaningful simulated altitude — is exactly the dose that **degrades sleep**. Hypoxia causes periodic breathing, arousals, reduced slow-wave sleep and fragmentation, worst in the first nights and increasing with simulated altitude. You are sabotaging recovery during the peak build phase of your training, in exchange for an unquantified benefit.

**Practical realities**: systems cost four figures to buy or several hundred a month to rent; the generator is noisy; the tent is hot and humid; and there is a household politics problem.

**The ceiling effect.** A tent addresses one variable. It cannot simulate the exertion, cold, dehydration, caloric deficit or sleep deprivation of an actual expedition.

> [!NOTE]
> If you are going to use a tent, use it the way the evidence points: long nightly exposures over several weeks, building gradually, with sleep quality monitored. Do not use it in the final taper. And do not let it substitute for a conservative ascent profile — no amount of pre-exposure changes the fact that ascent rate is the dominant variable.

## 6. Drugs

### Acetazolamide (Diamox) — first line, well evidenced

**Mechanism.** Carbonic anhydrase inhibition drives bicarbonate out in the urine, producing a mild metabolic acidosis that counteracts the alkalosis of hyperventilation and removes the chemical brake on breathing. Crucially, it **accelerates genuine acclimatization** rather than masking symptoms.

**Doses (WMS 2024):** prevention **125 mg every 12 hours**; treatment **250 mg every 12 hours**. Start the day before ascent, though it still helps if started on the day. Continue two days after arrival at your target elevation, or 2–4 days if ascending faster than recommended.

**Effect size.** A 2012 *BMJ* meta-analysis found a pooled odds ratio of **0.36** (95% CI 0.28–0.46). At 250 mg/day the number needed to treat is **6**. Higher doses showed no significant added benefit — which is why 125 mg twice daily became standard.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The WMS adds an under-appreciated caveat: the 125 mg dose applies to low-to-moderate risk profiles, 250 mg twice daily should be considered for high-risk profiles, and **"the appropriate dose for ascent above 5000 m is not clear."** If you are heading to a 6,000 m peak, that uncertainty applies to you.

**Side effects.** Paraesthesia (tingling in fingers, toes and around the mouth) is extremely common and the main reason people stop. Also polyuria, taste distortion — carbonated drinks and beer taste metallic and flat — and nausea. **Contraindicated** in anyone with a prior anaphylactic reaction to a sulfonamide or a history of Stevens-Johnson syndrome; cross-reaction risk in ordinary sulfa allergy is described as extremely low. Note also that acetazolamide is on the **WADA prohibited list** as a masking agent, which matters if you compete.

### Dexamethasone — effective, but it does not acclimatize you

**Doses (WMS 2024):** prevention **2 mg every 6 hours or 4 mg every 12 hours**; AMS treatment **4 mg every 6 hours**; HACE **8 mg once, then 4 mg every 6 hours**. Taper over a week if used more than 5–7 days.

The critical distinction: **dexamethasone does not facilitate acclimatization.** It suppresses symptoms. That has three consequences — further ascent must be delayed until you are asymptomatic *off* the drug, abrupt cessation risks rebound, and it masks the signal you most need to read.

> [!WARNING]
> Dexamethasone is a drug for **getting down**, not for going up. The WMS reserves prophylactic use for very high-risk situations such as rescue personnel airlifted above 3,500 m with immediate physical activity. It is not an expedition-long prophylactic.

### Ibuprofen — real, but second-tier

A 2019 trial compared ibuprofen 600 mg three times daily against acetazolamide 125 mg twice daily on rapid ascent to 3,810 m. AMS incidence was **62.2% on ibuprofen versus 51.1% on acetazolamide** — ibuprofen failed to demonstrate non-inferiority. Saturation was also lower (85.6% versus 88.5%).

The WMS position: ibuprofen cannot be recommended over acetazolamide or dexamethasone for prevention, but is reasonable for people who cannot or will not take those. Ibuprofen 600 mg every 8 hours **is** listed as treatment for high-altitude headache.

### Nifedipine and tadalafil — for HAPE-susceptible people only

The WMS is explicit: pharmacological HAPE prophylaxis should only be considered for individuals **with a history of HAPE, especially multiple episodes**.

- **Nifedipine ER 30 mg every 12 hours** (or 20 mg every 8 hours), from the day before ascent, continued 4 days at target elevation
- **Tadalafil 10 mg every 12 hours** as an alternative. There is **no role for using both together**
- Sildenafil 50 mg every 8 hours is listed as an option

## 7. What Does Not Work

The WMS 2024 guidelines dispatch an entire industry in one sentence, listing interventions **not** shown to be of benefit:

> chewed coca leaves, coca tea and other coca-derived products; "forced" or "over" hydration; short-term oxygen use in the form of either visits to oxygen bars or over-the-counter oxygen delivery systems by which individuals inhale oxygen-enriched gas from a small pre-filled canister; other over-the-counter products, such as powdered drink mixes, patches, and oral supplements.

**Ginkgo biloba** deserves its own mention because it was widely believed to work. The 2004 PHAIT trial in Himalayan trekkers settled it:

| Group | AMS incidence |
|---|---|
| Acetazolamide | **12%** |
| Ginkgo | 35% |
| Placebo | 34% |
| Combination | 14% |

Ginkgo versus placebo: odds ratio 0.95 (95% CI 0.56–1.62) — **no effect**. The earlier positive trials are generally attributed to unstandardised extract potency, which is a good lesson about supplements in general.

**Antioxidants** show only a non-significant trend (relative risk around 0.73, 95% CI 0.47–1.11) with high heterogeneity.

> [!CAUTION]
> "Forced hydration" is not benign. Overdrinking causes hyponatraemia, whose early symptoms — headache, nausea, confusion — are indistinguishable from AMS, and whose treatment is the opposite. Drink to thirst. Urine colour does not indicate hydration status at altitude, though a volume of at least a litre a day is a reasonable check.

## 8. Iron: The One Blood Test Worth Considering

Two separate mechanisms, worth keeping apart.

**Iron as substrate for red cell production.** Erythropoiesis is iron-dependent. Athletes with low ferritin (roughly under 20 ng/mL in women, 30 ng/mL in men) showed **no significant increase in red cell volume or {{< katex >}}\dot{V}O_{2}\text{max}{{< /katex >}}** after four weeks at moderate altitude, while iron-replete athletes did. Repletion restored the response.

**Iron and pulmonary blood pressure.** This is the more mountaineering-relevant strand. The enzymes that regulate the hypoxia-sensing HIF pathway require iron as a cofactor, so iron status directly tunes the oxygen-sensing machinery. Oxford work published in *JAMA* (2009) showed intravenous iron **attenuated** the hypoxia-induced rise in pulmonary artery pressure, and that deliberate iron depletion **augmented** it. Since excessive hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction is the core mechanism of HAPE, the implication is obvious — though untested in trekkers.

**Is testing worthwhile?** Pragmatically yes: iron deficiency is common, testing is cheap relative to an expedition, and correction takes 8–12 weeks so it must be done early. Test **ferritin, transferrin saturation and CRP** together — ferritin is an acute-phase reactant and inflammation falsely elevates it.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> No guideline recommends iron testing for altitude, and there is **no trial showing that correcting mild iron deficiency reduces AMS or HAPE in trekkers**. This is mechanistically well-supported and clinically untested. Ferritin under 30 ng/mL with low transferrin saturation is worth correcting regardless of altitude; under 15 ng/mL is unambiguous deficiency. Above roughly 100 ng/mL there is no rationale for supplementation, and iron overload carries its own risks. **Do not take iron blind.**

## 9. How Long Does Acclimatization Last?

Two processes decay at different rates.

**Ventilatory acclimatization** develops over days to weeks and decays over **days to about a week** after descent, though not completely.

**Haemoglobin mass** decays more slowly, over roughly three weeks, back toward baseline.

The **AltitudeOmics** project tested this directly: subjects acclimatized at 5,260 m, returned to low altitude, then re-ascended after 7 and after 21 days. Re-ascent produced **less illness and better oxygenation** than the initial ascent, with meaningful retention at 7 days and reduced but non-zero retention at 21 days.

Practically:

- **Under a week:** most acclimatization retained. This is the physiological basis of the classic Himalayan rotation — climb high, descend to base camp, re-ascend.
- **1–3 weeks:** partial retention, decaying. Resting at Namche or base camp preserves far more than flying home.
- **Over 3–4 weeks:** plan as though you are unacclimatized again.

> [!NOTE]
> The decay is faster than the acquisition. **Two ten-day trips separated by six weeks do not equal one twenty-day trip.** If you are stacking objectives in a season, the gaps matter as much as the trips.

## 10. Fitness

The core finding is well replicated and counter-intuitive: **sea-level aerobic fitness does not protect against altitude illness.**

Schneider's 827-mountaineer cohort found training status was not a significant determinant of AMS. More strikingly, in Richalet's risk-prediction score, **"regular physical activity" carries a positive weight — being a regular endurance trainer *increases* predicted risk of severe altitude illness.**

The explanation is probably behavioural as much as physiological. Fit people ascend faster, exert themselves harder, and tolerate more desaturation before they slow down. Some also have blunted chemosensitivity, and highly trained athletes desaturate more during hard exercise in the first place.

The evidence is not entirely one-sided — one large 1993 tourist study found poor self-reported fitness associated with more AMS — but that was cross-sectional, self-reported, and confounded with obesity, age and lung disease.

**What fitness does do** is determine how fast and far you can move, your reserve on long summit days, your load-carrying ability, and your margin for self-rescue. That is not nothing. It is just not immunity.

**Training that actually helps:**

1. **High aerobic base volume** — the dominant determinant of multi-day durability
2. **Long, load-carrying, vertical-gain-specific work** — weighted uphill hiking, and eccentric descent tolerance
3. **Strength work** for injury resistance, especially quadriceps for descending
4. **Back-to-back long days**, to train recovery under fatigue
5. **Fuelling practice** — appetite suppression and caloric deficit are major limiters up high
6. **Not hypoxic training sessions.** Training in hypoxia lowers your absolute training intensity for no demonstrated illness benefit. It makes you fitter more slowly.

> [!TIP]
> "Fit enough to move slowly for a very long time" beats "fit enough to move fast." And neither buys immunity from altitude illness.

## The Bottom Line

Rank the interventions by evidence, and the list is almost the inverse of the marketing:

1. **Conservative ascent profile.** Dominates everything else by roughly a factor of eight in Schneider's data. Free.
2. **Staged ascent — days at 2,200–3,000 m before going higher.** Cuts AMS incidence roughly in half. Nearly free.
3. **Acetazolamide 125 mg twice daily**, started the day before. Odds ratio 0.36, number needed to treat 6. Cheap, but a prescription drug with real side effects.
4. **Iron repletion if you are deficient.** Mechanistically sound, clinically untested for altitude, cheap to check.
5. **Hypoxic tent at more than 8 hours a night for weeks.** Plausible, unquantified, expensive, and it costs you sleep.
6. **Everything else** — short IHE sessions, hypoxic training, ginkgo, antioxidants, coca, oxygen cans, patches, powders. No credible evidence.

The uncomfortable summary is that the most effective thing you can do about altitude is to **go up more slowly**, and the second most effective is to **arrive already a bit high**. Everything you can buy is downstream of those two.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a hypoxic tent worth the money?

Probably not, for most people, most of the time. There is no randomised trial showing a tent protocol reduces altitude illness on a subsequent expedition, and the WMS declines to endorse any protocol. The exposure that might plausibly help — over 8 hours a night for several weeks — is also the exposure that fragments your sleep during the most important training block before a trip.

Where it makes more sense is when a conservative ascent profile is genuinely impossible: a short trip with a hard summit deadline, an itinerary where you fly straight to a high airport, or a repeat objective where you already know you struggle. Even then, treat it as a supplement to a sensible profile, not a replacement for one. If the same money buys you three extra acclimatization days on the mountain, buy the days.

### Should I take Diamox prophylactically, or wait and see?

That depends on your itinerary risk, not your ego. The evidence for prophylaxis is good — odds ratio 0.36, number needed to treat 6 — and the drug accelerates real acclimatization rather than masking symptoms. The WMS reserves it for moderate-to-high-risk ascent profiles, which broadly means rapid ascent, high sleeping altitudes, or a personal history of altitude illness.

The argument for waiting is that if you are ascending slowly on a conservative profile, your baseline risk may already be low enough that the side effects — the tingling, the polyuria, the ruined taste of everything carbonated — outweigh the benefit. The argument against waiting is that acetazolamide works better as prevention than as rescue. A reasonable middle path used by many is to carry it, start it if the profile compresses or symptoms appear, and discuss the decision with a clinician before you leave rather than improvising at 4,000 m.

### I did fine at 5,000 m last year. Does that mean I'll be fine again?

Partly, but less than you think. A history of tolerating altitude is a genuine favourable signal, and prior AMS is one of the strongest predictors of future AMS. But the WMS adds a caveat that cuts both ways: a history of altitude illness does not necessarily reflect high risk on all future ascents, because a slower profile or lower target may avoid problems entirely.

The inverse is also true. Your good trip may have been a slow profile, good weather, no illness, and adequate sleep. Change any of those and the outcome changes. In Schneider's data, susceptibility roughly doubled risk, while ascent strategy moved it by a factor of eight. Most repeat altitude illness is repeat behaviour, not repeat physiology.

### How much altitude do I keep between trips?

Enough to matter for about a week, decaying substantially by three. Under a week, most of your ventilatory acclimatization and nearly all of your haemoglobin gain persists — which is exactly why Himalayan expeditions use rotations rather than a single push. Between one and three weeks you retain a partial benefit that keeps shrinking. Past three or four weeks, plan as though you are starting over.

The practical consequence is that where you spend your rest matters enormously. Descending to Namche or base camp between rotations preserves far more than flying home. And because decay is faster than acquisition, splitting a long acclimatization into two separate trips is a poor substitute for one continuous one.

### Does training in a mask or a hypoxic room help?

Not for altitude illness, no. Exercise-in-hypoxia protocols run 30–90 minutes a session, which is an order of magnitude below any plausible acclimatization dose, and there is essentially no evidence that they prevent AMS. That literature exists to serve athletic performance, not mountaineering.

Worse, there is a real cost. Training in hypoxia forces you to lower your absolute workload — less power, less speed, less load — for the same perceived effort. Over a training block that means you get less fit than you would have training normally. For a mountaineer, whose limiting factor is usually how long they can move under load, that is a bad trade. Train hard at sea level; do your acclimatizing at altitude.
